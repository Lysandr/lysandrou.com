The Linear Quadratic Regulator

2026-04-24 · ~12 min read
Control Theory LQR Optimization

The Linear Quadratic Regulator is one of the most practically useful results in control theory — it gives you an optimal state-feedback gain in closed form, as long as your system is linear and your cost is quadratic. For GNC work especially, it shows up everywhere: attitude control, reference tracking, gain scheduling baselines. This post derives it from scratch, builds intuition for the weight matrices, and works through the double integrator as a concrete example.

Problem Setup

Consider a continuous-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system:

$$\dot{x}(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t), \quad x(0) = x_0$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the state and $u \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the control input. We want to steer $x \to 0$ while minimizing the cost of doing so. The infinite-horizon quadratic cost is:

$$J = \int_0^\infty \left( x(t)^T Q\, x(t) + u(t)^T R\, u(t) \right) dt$$

The weight matrices encode what we care about: $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, $Q \succeq 0$ penalizes state deviation, and $R \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$, $R \succ 0$ penalizes control effort. We need $R$ to be strictly positive definite so the problem is well-posed — infinite control effort must be infinitely expensive.

Existence conditions The optimal solution exists and is unique when $(A, B)$ is stabilizable (unstable modes can be controlled) and $(A, Q^{1/2})$ is detectable (unstable modes are visible in the cost). If these hold, the closed-loop system is guaranteed to be asymptotically stable.

Derivation via the HJB Equation

The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation is the fundamental optimality condition for continuous-time optimal control. For our infinite-horizon problem, the HJB requires finding a value function $V : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying:

$$0 = \min_{u} \left[ x^T Q x + u^T R u + \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} (Ax + Bu) \right]$$

We guess that the value function is quadratic: $V(x) = x^T P x$ for some symmetric $P \succ 0$. Then $\partial V / \partial x = 2P x$, and the HJB becomes:

$$0 = \min_{u} \left[ x^T Q x + u^T R u + 2x^T P (Ax + Bu) \right]$$

Minimizing over $u$, take the gradient with respect to $u$ and set to zero:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left[ u^T R u + 2x^T P B u \right] = 2Ru + 2B^T P x = 0$$

Since $R \succ 0$, this is a strictly convex problem in $u$, so the minimizer is unique:

$$u^*(t) = -R^{-1} B^T P\, x(t) \equiv -Kx(t)$$

where $K \triangleq R^{-1} B^T P$ is the optimal feedback gain matrix. Substituting $u^*$ back into the HJB and requiring it to hold for all $x$:

$$A^T P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^T P + Q = 0$$

This is the Algebraic Riccati Equation (ARE) — the central object of LQR. Solve for $P$, compute $K$, and you're done.

The Algebraic Riccati Equation

The ARE is a quadratic matrix equation in $P$. In general it has multiple solutions, but there is a unique stabilizing solution $P \succeq 0$ — the one that makes $A - BK$ stable. This is the solution we want.

Summary — LQR optimal control law

Given $(A, B, Q, R)$, solve the ARE for the stabilizing $P \succeq 0$:

$$A^T P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^T P + Q = 0$$

Compute the gain:

$$K = R^{-1} B^T P$$

Apply $u^*(t) = -Kx(t)$. The closed-loop system $\dot{x} = (A - BK)x$ is asymptotically stable, and $J^* = x_0^T P x_0$.

Note that $J^* = x_0^T P x_0$: the optimal cost is a quadratic function of the initial condition, and $P$ encodes how "hard" each direction in state space is to regulate. Eigenvectors of $P$ with large eigenvalues correspond to expensive-to-control directions.

Closed-Loop Dynamics

With the optimal control applied, the closed-loop system becomes:

$$\dot{x} = (A - BK)x = A_{cl}\, x$$

The ARE guarantees all eigenvalues of $A_{cl}$ lie strictly in the open left half-plane (under the stabilizability/detectability conditions). The closed-loop poles are not arbitrary — they depend on the ratio of $Q$ to $R$. In the scalar case this is intuitive: a large $Q/R$ ratio pushes poles further left (faster response, higher cost).

The return difference equality gives a useful robustness result. LQR is guaranteed to have at least 60° phase margin and infinite gain margin in the single-input case — a remarkable property not shared by pole placement or other design methods.

Choosing Q and R

Weight selection is where engineering judgment lives. The matrices $Q$ and $R$ are design parameters — there's no single right answer, only tradeoffs.

Bryson's Rule

A standard starting point. Normalize by the maximum acceptable deviation for each state and control:

$$Q_{ii} = \frac{1}{x_{i,\text{max}}^2}, \qquad R_{jj} = \frac{1}{u_{j,\text{max}}^2}$$

This non-dimensionalizes the cost and puts all states and inputs on equal footing. Treat the result as an initial guess, then tune from here based on simulated closed-loop behavior.

Scaling intuition

Multiplying $Q$ by a scalar $\alpha > 1$ (or dividing $R$ by $\alpha$) increases the cost of state error relative to control effort — the controller becomes more aggressive, uses more actuator authority, and achieves faster convergence. Poles move further left. The reverse shrinks control authority and slows the response.

Off-diagonal terms in $Q$ are less common but valid — they penalize combinations of states, which can be useful when you care about a derived quantity like $\delta\theta - \delta\omega / \omega_n$ in a pointing problem.

Solving the ARE in Practice

The ARE has a closed-form solution in terms of eigendecomposition of the Hamiltonian matrix:

$$\mathcal{H} = \begin{bmatrix} A & -BR^{-1}B^T \\ -Q & -A^T \end{bmatrix}$$

The Hamiltonian has $2n$ eigenvalues that come in stable/unstable pairs $\{\lambda_i, -\lambda_i\}$. The stabilizing solution $P$ is constructed from the $n$ stable eigenvectors (those with negative real part). Modern numerics handle this via Schur decomposition rather than direct eigendecomposition.

In code:

# Python (scipy)
from scipy.linalg import solve_continuous_are
import numpy as np

P = solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R)
K = np.linalg.solve(R, B.T @ P)   # K = R⁻¹ BᵀP
% MATLAB
[K, P, poles] = lqr(A, B, Q, R);

Example: Double Integrator

The canonical example — a unit mass on a frictionless surface, controlled by force $u$:

$$\dot{x} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} x + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{B} u$$

where $x = [p,\; \dot{p}]^T$ is position and velocity. Choose $Q = I_2$, $R = 1$. The ARE becomes:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} P + P \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} - P \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} P + I = 0$$

Write $P = \begin{bmatrix} p_1 & p_2 \\ p_2 & p_3 \end{bmatrix}$ (symmetric). Expanding and matching entries gives three scalar equations. Solving:

$$p_3 = \sqrt{1 + 2p_2}, \quad p_2 = \sqrt{p_3}, \quad p_1 = p_2 p_3$$

The numerical solution (with $Q = I$, $R = 1$) is:

$$P \approx \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 1 \\ 1 & \sqrt[4]{3} \cdot \sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} \quad \Rightarrow \quad K \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \sqrt[4]{12} \end{bmatrix}$$

More precisely, $K = [1,\ 3^{1/4}\sqrt{2}] \approx [1,\ 1.86]$. The closed-loop poles are:

$$\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{-(1 + \sqrt{3}) \pm \sqrt{(1+\sqrt{3})^2 - 4}}{2} \approx -0.63 \pm 0.90i$$

Both in the LHP — the system is stable. Increasing $Q$ (or decreasing $R$) moves these poles further left, giving faster settling at the cost of larger control inputs.

Discrete-Time LQR

For sampled-data systems $x_{k+1} = A_d x_k + B_d u_k$, the infinite-horizon cost is:

$$J = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left( x_k^T Q x_k + u_k^T R u_k \right)$$

The discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation (DARE) is:

$$P = Q + A_d^T P A_d - A_d^T P B_d \left(R + B_d^T P B_d\right)^{-1} B_d^T P A_d$$

and the optimal gain is:

$$K_d = \left(R + B_d^T P B_d\right)^{-1} B_d^T P A_d$$

In practice, digitize your plant (zero-order hold or Tustin), solve the DARE, and apply $u_k = -K_d x_k$ at each sample. The continuous-time robustness margins don't fully carry over to discrete time, so verify your margins explicitly when the sample rate is low relative to the bandwidth. 

# Python (scipy)
from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_are

P  = solve_discrete_are(Ad, Bd, Q, R)
Kd = np.linalg.solve(R + Bd.T @ P @ Bd, Bd.T @ P @ Ad)

Limitations and Extensions

LQR is a powerful baseline, but it has real limitations worth keeping in mind:

  • Full-state feedback required. LQR assumes you can measure $x$ directly. If you can't, pair it with a Kalman filter — this gives you the LQG controller, which is optimal under Gaussian noise.
  • No explicit constraint handling. LQR doesn't enforce actuator saturation or state limits. Use MPC if hard constraints matter.
  • Nonlinear systems. Linearize around an operating point, design LQR on the linearization, and schedule gains if the operating range is large. For strongly nonlinear problems, iLQR or DDP are natural extensions.
  • Weight tuning. Despite Bryson's rule, getting good performance often requires iteration. Simulate first; don't tune gains on hardware.
